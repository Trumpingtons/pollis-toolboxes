# Writing the Choices

The wrong choices (distractors) decide what an exercise teaches. A distractor that nobody picks tests nothing; one that comes from a real mistake tells the student, and the teacher, which mistake was made.

## Distractors from Usual Mistakes

Compute each wrong choice from the same data as the correct one, with the mistake built in:

| Correct | Usual mistake |
|---|---|
| Sample standard deviation | Dividing by $n$ instead of $n - 1$; forgetting the square root |
| Median of an even number of values | Taking one of the two middle values |
| Two-sided p-value | The one-sided value; the normal instead of the t distribution |
| Slope of $y$ on $x$ | Dividing by the variance of $y$; the correlation |
| Prediction $b_0 + b_1 x_0$ | Leaving out the intercept |

Three or four choices are enough. Avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above": they test the format, not the topic.

## Choices That Never Coincide

Two choices that come out the same make a version ambiguous. It happens when a mistake does not matter for some data: the mean and the median of a symmetric sample, the slope of $y$ on $x$ and of $x$ on $y$ when both variances are equal. Remedies:

- Draw the data so that the cases where they coincide are rare, then check many versions.
- Round every choice to the same number of decimals: equal after rounding is equal to the student.
- If some data make two choices equal, draw the data again with the next seed.

## The Order of the Choices

Write the correct choice first and let the version shuffle them, so that the correct one is spread evenly over the letters and its position gives nothing away.
