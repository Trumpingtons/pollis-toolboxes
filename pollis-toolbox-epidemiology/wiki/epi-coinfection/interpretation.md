# Interpretation

## Reading the Sign of an Interaction

An entry above 1 means that carrying one strain makes a host more likely to catch the other, and an entry below 1 means less likely. Each direction is separate, as one strain can help the other and be hindered in turn. The population result does not always follow the sign of the entry: a strain helped by another can still lose if the helper uses up hosts. Next Steps > What the Interactions Change counts infected host-steps with and without the interactions, and shows the net effect.

## Competition Without Any Within-Host Interaction

Strains can interfere at the population level without acting on each other in the host at all. A fatal or a debilitating disease takes hosts out of the pool available to others, as [Rohani, Green, Mantilla-Beniers and Grenfell (2003)](#ref-RGMG2003a) showed for childhood diseases. Ecological interference of this kind appears here with Disease Mortality, which is one reason that a run without interactions is the baseline, and not an assumption of independence.

## What Order of Arrival Says

A strain that arrives first can change who is left for a later one. Under an asymmetric matrix, the outcome can then depend on order, a priority effect. Next Steps > Order of Arrival shows this for two strains. When arrival order matters, the history of a host population helps to explain which strains persist, an idea from parasite community ecology ([Pedersen and Fenton (2007)](#ref-PF2007a)).

## Why Parasite Communities Are Studied as Communities

Risk of one infection can depend more on the other infections a host carries than on host age or season, as a long study of wild voles found ([Telfer, Lambin, Birtles, Beldomenico, Burthe, Paterson and Begon (2010)](#ref-TLBBB...2010a)). Two-strain results do not always carry over to communities, where indirect effects travel through a third strain. An effect that vanishes with no third strain can only be seen in the Strain Community tab.

## What Simulated Coinfection Levels Say About Real Ones

Levels from a simulation show what a mechanism can produce, and not what is happening in a real population. If a mechanism in a simulation produces a pattern like the real one, it remains a candidate among others, particularly since differences among hosts can produce similar patterns. A real claim requires data able to separate these explanations, for which [Shrestha, King and Rohani (2011)](#ref-SKR2011a) show how much data can be needed.
